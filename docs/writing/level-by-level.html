Writing a paper, level by level

structure
clarity
evidence
A method for clear scientific writing, from the whole paper down to a single word, with the evidence behind each rule.
Published

October 23, 2026

Each level of a paper holds one thing. A paper carries one message, a section does one job, a paragraph develops one idea, and a sentence adds one new point. Plan and revise from the outside in: a perfect sentence inside a paragraph you later cut is wasted effort.

Paper 1 message
Section 1 job
Paragraph 1 idea
Sentence 1 new point
Word 1 term per concept

The same pattern repeats at every scale: context, then content, then conclusion. Every rule below carries a label for how strong its evidence is: Experimental Correlational Descriptive Expert. They are explained at the end.

The workflow

  1. Message. Make the figures, then write the take-home message in one or two sentences. Leave it a day, then revise it.
  2. Skeleton. Put the figures in order. Draft the five pivotal paragraphs first (see Section), then one topic sentence for every other paragraph.
  3. Draft. Write fast and do not polish. A common order is Methods, Results, Discussion, Introduction, Abstract, Title.
  4. Revise from the top down. Paper, sections, paragraphs, sentences, words.
  5. Test with a reader. Someone outside your subfield restates the message in their own words.

To plan before drafting, the paper brief turns stages 1 and 2 into a list of questions.

Paper

Goal: the reader finishes with one message they can repeat.

Write

  1. Write the take-home message in one sentence: what the result changes, not only what you found.
  2. Write the story as And, But, Therefore.
  3. Choose the three to five figures that carry the message and order them as an argument.
  4. Keep hypotheses, predictions, analyses, results and discussion points in the same order everywhere.
  5. Write a working title that states the finding, not the topic.

Check

  • Can a colleague restate the message after reading only the abstract?
  • Does every section serve the message? What does not goes to the supplement or out.
  • If the Introduction lists three predictions, do Methods, Results and Discussion follow the same three, in the same order?
  • Is any conclusion stronger than the results support?

Example (invented, on a mountain pine beetle forecasting topic)

And mountain pine beetle outbreaks have killed pine over large areas of western Canada, and managers must decide where to act a year in advance. But most outbreak maps describe where beetles are now, not where they will spread next. Therefore we built a model that forecasts spread one year ahead and tested it against past outbreak records.

A result

Beetle spread was lower after winters with colder minimum temperatures.

A take-home message

Forecasts of next year’s spread should weight winter minimum temperature more than current models do.

A result says what you found; a take-home message says what it changes. The same goes for titles: “Investigating the dynamics of beetle spread using a forecasting framework” names a topic, while “Cold winters slow beetle spread the following year” states a finding. Use the second kind only when your results support it.

Evidence: Expert for the steps (Fahrig; Magnusson 1996; Mensh and Kording 2017; Olson 2015). Correlational more narrative abstracts and short, result-stating titles are cited more (Hillier et al. 2016; Paiva et al. 2012). Experimental result-stating titles did not change how strongly readers believed the findings (Research Integrity and Peer Review, 2016), so they help readers find a paper rather than persuade them.

Section

Goal: each section does one job, and together they form an hourglass.

Section Its job
Abstract The whole story in small: context, gap, approach, main result with numbers, implication.
Introduction From the broad problem, to what is known, to the gap, ending with the aim of this paper.
Methods Enough to reproduce the work, in the same order as the Results.
Results One finding per paragraph. Each paragraph opens with the finding, not the method.
Discussion The answer first, then how it fits current knowledge, limitations, and implications.

The numbered pins are the five pivotal paragraphs (McGill 2016): (1) the big question, (2) this paper’s question, (3) the main answer, (4) back to the big question, and (5) the abstract. Draft them first; the rest fills in between.

Check

  • Read only the first sentence of each paragraph. Do they tell the story alone?
  • Does the Introduction end on exactly the question the Discussion answers, stated without methods?
  • Are the limitations in the middle of the Discussion? The first and last paragraphs carry the emphasis.
  • Does the Discussion add meaning, or repeat the Results?

Opens with the method

We fitted a generalized additive model with winter minimum temperature as a predictor of spread.

Opens with the finding

Beetles spread less after colder winters (Fig. 2). We tested this with a generalized additive model…

Evidence: Expert for structure (McGill 2016; Mensh and Kording 2017; Heard 2016). Experimental headings improve readers’ memory of a text’s topic structure (Lorch et al. 2013).

Paragraph

Goal: one idea, stated first, then developed, with each sentence linked to the one before.

Part What it does
Link (optional) A few words tying this paragraph to the last: “In contrast”, “This pattern”.
Topic sentence The paragraph’s point, stated as a claim. Skimming first sentences should give the argument.
Support, 3–4 sentences Evidence, reasoning, citations, figure references. Each opens with something the reader already has.
Close (optional) What the point means, or a hand-off to the next paragraph. Not a summary.

In the 148 research papers I measured from my own reading library, a typical paragraph ran about 160 words and five to six sentences.

Example (invented numbers). Shading marks information the reader already has and what each sentence adds:

Forecasts of beetle spread need an accurate map of current infestations. These maps come mainly from annual aerial surveys. Aerial surveys, however, record trees attacked the previous year, so every forecast starts one year behind the outbreak.

Each sentence picks up what the previous one ended on, then adds one new thing at its end.

Check

  • Can you sum up the paragraph in one line? If you need two, split it.
  • Do the sentence subjects form a chain the reader can follow?
  • Do transitions show logic (because, however, therefore) rather than just add (moreover, additionally)?
  • Who reads this paragraph? In the abstract and introduction, state every link; in technical methods, specialists can fill small gaps.

Evidence: Experimental putting known information before new speeds comprehension (Haviland and Clark 1974), and how much to spell out depends on the reader’s background (McNamara et al. 1996). Expert for topic sentences (Fahrig; Heard 2016).

Sentence

Goal: the reader understands the sentence on the first read.

Position What goes there
Start (topic position) Old information: who or what the sentence is about.
Subject and verb The character and its action, early and close together.
End (stress position) The new, important point. Readers weight it most.

Write

  1. Find the main character and the action.
  2. Make them the subject and verb, close together, near the start.
  3. Put the new, important information at the end.
  4. Split the sentence if it carries more than one new idea.
  5. For a result, state its direction and size, not just that groups differed.

Before

An analysis of the relationship between winter minimum temperature and the survival of overwintering larvae was performed.

After

We tested whether colder winters reduced the survival of overwintering larvae.

Before

Stand age was the strongest predictor of attack, according to our model.

After

In our model, the strongest predictor of attack was stand age.

Before

Attack rates differed between young and mature stands (Table 1).

After

Mature stands were attacked about twice as often as young stands (Table 1).

Evidence: Experimental readers recalled important ideas better when actions were verbs rather than nouns (Spyridakis and Isakson 1998) and misread passive sentences more often than active ones (Ferreira 2003). Experimental keeping linked words close lowers processing cost in controlled sentences (Gibson 1998), but in natural reading the effect appeared only at nouns (Demberg and Keller 2008): mixed. Expert for stress position (Gopen and Swan 1990; Williams and Bizup).

Word

Goal: each word is precise, simple, and used the same way throughout.

  1. Choose one term per concept and keep it. Keep a term list for the paper.
  2. Cut jargon and acronyms where you can; define the rest at first use.
  3. Prefer concrete words: organisms, places, numbers, processes.
  4. Match hedges to the strength of the evidence.
  5. Cut filler.
Instead of Write Why
utilize use Same meaning, shorter.
in order to to Filler.
it is important to note that (cut) If it matters, the sentence shows it.
may potentially suggest may suggest One hedge, matched to the evidence. Do not cut the last one.
outbreak … epidemic … infestation outbreak, every time Synonyms make readers think you mean different things.
spatiotemporal dynamics where and when beetles spread Concrete over abstract.

Evidence: Experimental needlessly complex words lowered readers’ opinion of the writer (Oppenheimer 2006); jargon lowered processing fluency even when definitions were given (Bullock et al. 2019); hedged news stories made scientists seem more trustworthy to lay readers (Jensen 2008). Correlational more jargon in titles and abstracts went with fewer citations (Martínez and Mammola 2021). Descriptive 79% of acronyms in the literature are used fewer than ten times (Barnett and Doubleday 2020).

How big is each part?

Count the jobs first, then check the length. The sizes below are what is typical in the papers I read, not targets.

Introduction~1,000 words · 18%5–7 paragraphs
Methods~1,600 words · 30%7–15 paragraphs
Results~900 words · 18%5–10 paragraphs
Discussion~1,600 words · 30%7–12 paragraphs

Widths show each section’s median share of the text; each small box is one paragraph at the median; ranges are the middle half of papers. Descriptive

  1. Count the jobs. One paragraph per question, prediction, analysis or finding.
  2. Check the budget. Paragraphs ≈ section words ÷ 160.
  3. Check each paragraph. Over about 250 words, it probably holds two ideas. Under about 60 words, merge it or develop it.
Section Paragraph slots, in order
Introduction Big question (1) · what is known (2–4) · the gap (1) · question and numbered predictions (1)
Methods Overview · one per data source · one per model or measurement · one per analysis, in prediction order · software and code
Results Optional data overview · one or two per finding, finding first, in prediction order
Discussion The answer · one or two per finding · limitations (in the middle) · implications · back to the big question

A methods paper has different slots; the paper brief gives both versions.

The practice loop

Guides describe good writing; getting better at it takes practice. Work on one skill at a time, with quick feedback and repetition (Kellogg 2008).

  1. Pick one skill from your recurring problems: stress position, topic sentences, hidden verbs.
  2. Study a model: a short passage from a well-written paper that does it well, with what it does named.
  3. Rewrite a passage of your own applying that one skill.
  4. Get quick feedback on that skill only, then do one more round.
  5. Log the pattern in a personal style guide when a habit is fixed or keeps returning.

Evidence: studying models and sentence combining both improved writing in a meta-analysis of school students, so their transfer to research writing is assumed (Graham and Perin 2007). Peer feedback improved writing quality across 60 studies (Wei and Liu 2024). No schedule is prescribed: writing every day is not shown to beat other routines (Sword 2016).

How strong is this advice?

Label Meaning Example
Experimental Tested by changing a text and measuring readers. Most tests used short texts and student or lay readers. More accessible abstracts improved readers’ understanding and confidence (Freeling et al. 2021).
Correlational Links between writing features and citations. They show associations, not causes. More narrative abstracts were cited more (Hillier et al. 2016).
Descriptive Trends in the literature. Abstracts have become harder to read since 1881 (Plavén-Sigray et al. 2017).
Expert Advice from experienced writers and editors. It rests on how readers process text but is untested. Most of the structure above.

Readability formulas, which count word and sentence length, are not a measure of clarity: in 4,191 forestry abstracts they showed no link with citations (Jiang et al. 2026). Use sentence length only as a warning sign.
