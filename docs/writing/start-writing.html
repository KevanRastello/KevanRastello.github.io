Start writing: the paper brief

planning
structure
evidence
Answer these questions before drafting, and the outline of the paper writes itself. With versions for regular and methods papers.
Published

October 23, 2026

A blank page is hard; a list of questions is not. This brief asks the questions a paper has to answer, in an order that builds the outline as you go. Each answer feeds a specific paragraph, so a finished brief is a finished skeleton.

Download the brief to fill in: Word (.docx) · Markdown (.md)

How to use it:

  1. Choose the paper type (Step 0).
  2. Answer Blocks A to D in writing, in order.
  3. Pass the four gates.
  4. Turn each slot into a topic sentence, then draft paragraph by paragraph, using the level-by-level method.

Step 0: choose the paper type

Decide this first, because six of the questions change with the type. A regular paper answers a question about nature; a methods paper offers a way of doing something that others can reuse.

Regular paper Methods paper
Take-home message is about The system: species, forests, outbreaks The procedure: how to compare, measure, forecast
Introduction ends on A question and numbered predictions A gap in existing methods, independent of any study system
Core of the paper Results that answer the predictions The method, then its evaluation
Evidence Data from the study system Simulations across a range of conditions, comparison with alternatives, error rates
Case study Is the paper One short worked example, often a single figure
Discussion What the finding means for ecology or management When to use the method, its limits, how to implement it

Test 1. Draft the take-home message roughly. If its subject is the system, it is a regular paper; if its subject is a procedure, it is a methods paper.

Test 2. Would the paper still matter if the case study were a different species or region? If yes, it leans methods. A good methods paper is about the method, not the study system (Ellison 2023).

If a project holds both a new method and a new finding, two papers are often clearer than one. Methods journals also set the format: at Methods in Ecology and Evolution, a Research Article (a new general method) and a Workflow (a new pipeline of existing procedures) run to 8,000 words, and an Application or Practical Tool to 4,000.

Writing backwards: what it gains, what it costs

The brief starts from your results and works back to the question, following Magnusson’s “write backwards” method: conclusions first, then only the results, methods, discussion and introduction they need. It is worth using, but only with safeguards, because its main risk is quietly rewriting the question to fit the answer.

What happens Evidence
Gain: content before style The story is fixed first; style is easier to correct afterwards. Expert
Gain: report what matters You stop reporting every analysis in the order you ran it (Heard 2016). Expert
Gain: planning improves the text Outlining before drafting produced better documents than not outlining (Kellogg 1988). Experimental
Gain: an easier start You begin from what you know best: your results and figures. Expert
Cost: HARKing Writing the Introduction last invites presenting an unexpected finding as if it had been predicted (Kerr 1998). 51% of 807 ecologists and evolutionary biologists admitted doing so at least once (Fraser et al. 2018). Survey
Cost: selective reporting “Only the results the conclusions need” can slide into dropping inconvenient ones. 64% admitted not reporting non-significant results (Fraser et al. 2018). Survey
Cost: overselling Conclusion-first framing can push claims past the evidence. Expert
Cost: locking in too early Outlining improves text quality but can stop the new ideas that come from drafting, at least for some writers (Galbraith and Baaijen 2018). Experimental, early

The brief keeps the gains and blocks the costs with five safeguards:

  1. Record the original question first. Question C1 asks what you set out to test before you saw the results. Anything not in it is reported as exploratory.
  2. List the results that do not fit. Question B3 has a line for them; they stay in the paper.
  3. Calibrate claims. Each conclusion carries the hedge its evidence needs.
  4. Leave room to discover. If you cannot answer B1 yet, write a rough discussion freely first, outline what you wrote, then return to the brief.
  5. Change the plan for a reason. Revise the brief when a better story appears, but only if you can say why it is better (Heard 2016).

Block A: reader and venue

# Question Feeds
A1 Who is the reader? Name one real person outside your subfield who should understand the paper. How much each section spells out
A2 Target journal, article type and word limit. Paragraph budget = words ÷ 160. Paragraph count per section
A3 Regular or methods paper (Step 0)? One line on why. Which template and question versions

How much to spell out depends on what the reader already knows (McNamara et al. 1996), and experts misjudge what novices find hard (Hinds 1999). A named reader makes both concrete. Experimental

Block B: the answer first

# Question Feeds
B1 Your conclusions: at most five, one sentence each, no “however”. Methods paper: what the method does better than existing ones, with numbers. Discussion ¶1; abstract
B2 Your figures (about five at most) and tables (about four), in the order that best tells the story. One line each: what should the reader see? Results, one or two ¶ per figure
B3 The results each conclusion needs, with direction, size and figure. Then, separately: results that do not fit the conclusions. Methods paper: performance results (accuracy, error rates, sensitivity) and the comparison with alternatives. Results ¶; Discussion limitations
B4 The take-home message in one sentence: not what you found, but what it changes. Leave it a day, then revise. Last sentence of the abstract; Discussion ¶1; title
B5 The central question and its answer, in two sentences. Introduction last ¶; Discussion ¶1

From Magnusson (conclusions, then only the results they need), Heard (figure shuffling and the two-sentence mini-summary) and Fahrig (the take-home message as an implication). Expert

Block C: the question and the argument

Answer C1 from your notes or proposal, not from memory of the results.

# Question Feeds
C1 The original question and predictions, as they stood before you saw the results. Mark any that changed, and any new ones: those are reported as exploratory. Methods paper: the performance claims you set out to test. Introduction last ¶; the order of everything after it
C2 Numbered predictions, P1, P2…, in the order the paper will answer them. Introduction last ¶; Methods, Results and Discussion order
C3 The big question your field cares about, and your pitch: resolves a controversy · a gap that blocks a wider goal · blocks a practical problem · a model system for a wider field · a new path to a hard goal. Methods paper: the task researchers need to do, in any system. Introduction ¶1; Discussion last ¶
C4 What the reader must know first: two to four points, each with its key references. Methods paper: how the task is done now, and where that falls short. Introduction ¶2–4
C5 The gap and why it matters, without “no studies have” or “to increase our understanding”. Methods paper: the gap in methods, stated without your study system. Introduction gap ¶
C6 For each prediction: the data and analysis needed, and only those. Methods paper: the method’s components and assumptions, the simulation design, the alternatives compared, the case study. Methods ¶, in prediction order
C7 The story in three lines: And (context), But (gap), Therefore (what you did and found). Abstract; Introduction arc

From Heard (the story summary and the five pitches), Fahrig (flow-through order), McGill (the pivotal first paragraph), Olson (And, But, Therefore) and Ellison (methods papers). Expert

Block D: meaning and limits

# Question Feeds
D1 For each conclusion: does it agree with, extend or contradict earlier work? Name the papers. Discussion ¶2–5, in prediction order
D2 The limitations that could change a conclusion, and by how much. Methods paper: the method’s assumptions, and when someone should not use it. Discussion middle, never first or last
D3 What changes for management or theory? Methods paper: how to implement it; where the code and data are. Discussion implications ¶
D4 A working title that states the finding: short, no question mark. Methods paper: a title that says what the method does. Title
D5 What you are leaving out, and why: not relevant, redundant, or a separate paper. Conflicting results never go here. Supplement or another paper

Four gates before drafting

  • Message. B4 is one sentence, states an implication, and passes the Step 0 test. You left it a day and revised it. Your reader from A1 could repeat it.
  • Honesty. C1 came from your notes, not your results. Every prediction not in C1 is labelled exploratory. B3 lists the results that do not fit, and D5 hides none of them.
  • Order. The predictions in C2, figures in B2, analyses in C6 and comparisons in D1 all follow the same order.
  • Budget. The paragraph count from the tables below fits the A2 budget. If it is over, cut a prediction or move it to the supplement before writing.

Where each answer goes

When the gates pass, write one topic sentence per slot from its answers. That list is your skeleton. Then draft each paragraph as link, topic sentence, three or four supporting sentences, and close.
